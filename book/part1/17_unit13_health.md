## A · Grammar / Gramática

**EN:** In Unit 1 you met **helper verbs**: small words that work with the main verb and make a **two-part engine**. In Unit 12 you used one of them: *will*. This unit teaches the rest of the family: *can, could, should, must* — and one "impostor", *have to*. These helpers are called **modals**. Good news: after *do/does/did*, modals are the easiest helpers in English.

**ES:** En la Unidad 1 conociste los **verbos auxiliares**: palabras pequeñas que trabajan con el verbo principal y forman un **motor de dos partes**. En la Unidad 12 usaste uno de ellos: *will*. Esta unidad enseña el resto de la familia: *can, could, should, must* — y un "impostor", *have to*. Estos auxiliares se llaman **modales**. Buena noticia: después de *do/does/did*, los modales son los auxiliares más fáciles del inglés.

### A1 · What modals do / Qué hacen los modales

**EN:** A modal adds an **attitude** to the main verb: ability, permission, advice or obligation. The modal carries the attitude; the main verb carries the action. *I can swim* = ability + swim.

**ES:** Un modal le añade una **actitud** al verbo principal: habilidad, permiso, consejo u obligación. El modal lleva la actitud; el verbo principal lleva la acción. *I can swim* = habilidad + nadar.

| Modal | Job / Trabajo | Example | Español |
|---|---|---|---|
| **can** | ability, permission | I can drive. Can I sit here? | Sé manejar. ¿Puedo sentarme aquí? |
| **could** | past ability; polite request | Could you help me? | ¿Podría ayudarme? |
| **should** | advice / consejo | You should rest. | Deberías descansar. |
| **must** | strong obligation, a rule | You must wear a uniform. | Debes llevar uniforme. |
| **have to** | obligation (a boss, a rule) | I have to work on Saturday. | Tengo que trabajar el sábado. |

**EN:** Look at the seats. The modal and the main verb sit **together** in the verb seat. Two words, one engine:

**ES:** Mira los asientos. El modal y el verbo principal se sientan **juntos** en el asiento del verbo. Dos palabras, un motor:

| SUBJECT / SUJETO | VERB (modal + main) | OBJECT / REST | Español |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lucía | **can** make | arepas. | Lucía sabe hacer arepas. |
| Mateo | **should** drink | a lot of water. | Mateo debería tomar mucha agua. |
| You | **must** wear | a name tag. | Debes llevar una etiqueta con tu nombre. |
| Lucía | makes | arepas. | *(one-word engine / motor de una palabra)* |

**⚠ Spanish trap / Trampa del español:** for an ability you learned (*sé nadar, sé manejar*), English uses **can**, not *know*. ✗ *I know swim.* → ✓ *I can swim.* / Para una habilidad aprendida (*sé nadar, sé manejar*), el inglés usa **can**, no *know*.

### A2 · The three laws of modals / Las tres leyes de los modales

**EN:** Every true modal obeys three laws:

1. ★ **Modal + BASE verb.** No *to*, no *-ing*. ✗ *She can to swim.* → ✓ *She can **swim**.*
2. **No -s, ever** — not on the modal, not on the main verb. ✗ *She cans drive. She can drives.* → ✓ *She **can drive**.*
3. **No do / does / did.** Modals make their own negatives and questions, like *be* (Unit 2): negative = modal + *not*; question = flip the modal and the subject.

**ES:** Todo modal verdadero obedece tres leyes: (1) ★ **modal + verbo BASE** — sin *to*, sin *-ing*; (2) **sin -s, jamás** — ni en el modal ni en el verbo principal; (3) **sin do / does / did** — los modales hacen sus propias negaciones y preguntas, como *be* (Unidad 2): negación = modal + *not*; pregunta = invertir el modal y el sujeto.

| Form / Forma | can | should | Español |
|---|---|---|---|
| + | She can drive. | You should rest. | Sabe manejar. / Deberías descansar. |
| − | She can't drive. | You shouldn't go out. | No sabe manejar. / No deberías salir. |
| ? | Can she drive? | Should I call a doctor? | ¿Sabe manejar? / ¿Debería llamar a un médico? |
| short answer | Yes, she can. / No, she can't. | Yes, you should. / No, you shouldn't. | Sí. / No. |

**EN:** The negative forms: *can't* (= *cannot*, one word), *couldn't*, *shouldn't*, *mustn't*. In *mustn't*, the first *t* is silent: say "MAS-nt".

**ES:** Las formas negativas: *can't* (= *cannot*, en una sola palabra), *couldn't*, *shouldn't*, *mustn't*. En *mustn't*, la primera *t* no se pronuncia: di "MÁS-nt".

### A3 · Can and could / Can y could

**EN:** **Can** is for ability (*I can cook*) and permission (*Can I…?*). **Could** is the past of *can* (*I could swim at age six*), and it is also a **polite** way to ask for something (*Could you…?*). You will use *Could you…?* a lot in Unit 14.

**ES:** **Can** es para la habilidad (*I can cook*, sé cocinar) y el permiso (*Can I…?*, ¿puedo…?). **Could** es el pasado de *can* (*I could swim at age six*, sabía nadar a los seis años), y también es una forma **cortés** de pedir algo (*Could you…?*, ¿podría…?). Usarás mucho *Could you…?* en la Unidad 14.

| Job / Trabajo | Example | Español |
|---|---|---|
| ability now | Mateo can swim. | Mateo sabe nadar. |
| permission | Can I open the window? | ¿Puedo abrir la ventana? |
| not possible now | I can't come today. | No puedo venir hoy. |
| past ability | I could read at four. | Sabía leer a los cuatro años. |
| past, negative | Mateo couldn't sleep last night. | Mateo no pudo dormir anoche. |
| polite request | Could you repeat that, please? | ¿Podría repetir eso, por favor? |

**EN:** Listen for the difference: in *I can swim*, *can* is short and weak ("kn"). In *I can't swim*, *can't* is long and strong ("KANT"). Americans often don't say the final *t* clearly, so listen for the strong vowel.

**ES:** Escucha la diferencia: en *I can swim*, *can* es corto y débil ("kn"). En *I can't swim*, *can't* es largo y fuerte ("KANT"). Los estadounidenses muchas veces no pronuncian bien la *t* final, así que escucha la vocal fuerte.

### A4 · Should: advice / Should: consejos

**EN:** Use **should** to give advice: it says what is a **good idea**. *Shouldn't* says what is **not** a good idea. It is like Spanish *deberías*. To ask for advice, flip it: *Should I…?*

**ES:** Usa **should** para dar consejos: dice qué es una **buena idea**. *Shouldn't* dice qué **no** es buena idea. Es como el español *deberías*. Para pedir un consejo, inviértelo: *Should I…?* (¿Debería…?).

| SUBJECT / SUJETO | VERB (modal + main) | OBJECT / REST | Español |
|---|---|---|---|
| You | **should** see | a doctor. | Deberías ver a un médico. |
| You | **shouldn't** eat | a lot of sugar. | No deberías comer mucho azúcar. |
| Mateo | **should** stay | in bed. | Mateo debería quedarse en cama. |
| Ana | sees | a doctor every year. | *(one-word engine / motor de una palabra)* |

**⚠ Spanish trap / Trampa del español:** in Spanish, friends often say *"¡Tienes que ir al médico!"* as advice. In English, *You have to…* sounds like an order or a rule. For friendly advice, use *should*: ✓ *You should see a doctor.* / En español, los amigos dicen a menudo *"¡Tienes que ir al médico!"* como consejo. En inglés, *You have to…* suena a orden o a regla. Para un consejo amistoso, usa *should*.

### A5 · Must and have to: rules / Must y have to: reglas

**EN:** **Must** and **have to** both mean obligation: *tener que, deber*. *Must* is strong and is common in written rules and on signs. *Have to* is the everyday word for obligations from outside: a boss, a school, the law. In American English, people say *have to* much of the time.

**ES:** **Must** y **have to** significan obligación: *tener que, deber*. *Must* es fuerte y es común en reglas escritas y en letreros. *Have to* es la palabra de todos los días para obligaciones que vienen de afuera: un jefe, una escuela, la ley. En el inglés de Estados Unidos, la gente dice *have to* la mayor parte del tiempo.

**★ The exception / La excepción:** *have to* is **not** a true modal. It works like a normal verb, so it **takes -s** (*she has to*) and it **uses do / does / did** (Unit 5, Unit 11). *Must* has no past: use *had to*.

**ES:** *have to* **no** es un modal verdadero. Funciona como un verbo normal: **lleva -s** (*she has to*) y **usa do / does / did** (Unidades 5 y 11). *Must* no tiene pasado: usa *had to*.

| Form / Forma | Example | Español |
|---|---|---|
| + | Lucía has to work on Saturday. | Lucía tiene que trabajar el sábado. |
| − | She doesn't have to work on Sunday. | No tiene que trabajar el domingo. |
| ? | Does she have to wear a uniform? | ¿Tiene que llevar uniforme? |
| past | She had to leave early yesterday. | Tuvo que salir temprano ayer. |

**★ mustn't ≠ don't have to**

**EN:** In the negative, the two words mean very different things. *Mustn't* = it is **prohibited**. *Don't have to* = it is **not necessary** (but you can if you want).

**ES:** En negativo, las dos palabras significan cosas muy distintas. *Mustn't* = está **prohibido**. *Don't have to* = **no es necesario** (pero puedes hacerlo si quieres).

| Negative | Meaning / Significado | Example | Español |
|---|---|---|---|
| mustn't | prohibited / prohibido | You mustn't smoke here. | No debes fumar aquí. (Prohibido.) |
| don't have to | not necessary / no es necesario | You don't have to come. | No tienes que venir. (No hace falta.) |

**⚠ Spanish trap / Trampa del español:** Spanish *"no tienes que"* = ***don't have to***, NOT *mustn't*. ✗ *You mustn't come if you are busy.* → ✓ *You **don't have to** come.* / El español *"no tienes que"* = ***don't have to***, NO *mustn't*.

### A6 · Common mistakes / Errores comunes

| ✗ | ✓ | Why / Por qué |
|---|---|---|
| I can to drive. | I can **drive**. | law 1: base verb, no *to* |
| She cans cook. / She can cooks. | She **can cook**. | law 2: no -s anywhere |
| Do you can help me? | **Can you** help me? | law 3: flip the modal |
| I don't can swim. | I **can't** swim. | law 3: modal + not |
| He must works. | He must **work**. | base verb after a modal |
| She have to study. | She **has to** study. | *have to* is a normal verb → -s |
| She doesn't has to go. | She doesn't **have** to go. | after *doesn't* → base verb |
| You mustn't come. (= no need) | You **don't have to** come. | mustn't = prohibited |
| I know swim. | I **can** swim. | learned ability = *can* |

### Word tip / Consejo de palabras

**EN:** Remember the *tener* trap from Unit 2: *Tengo frío* = *I am cold*. But a **cold** is also an illness: *Tengo un resfriado* = *I **have** a cold*. So: *I am cold* (temperature) but *I have a cold* (illness). And a false friend: *constipated* does **not** mean *constipado* (with a cold). It means *estreñido*! For *estoy constipado/a*, say *I have a cold*.

**ES:** Recuerda la trampa de *tener* de la Unidad 2: *Tengo frío* = *I am cold*. Pero **a cold** también es una enfermedad: *Tengo un resfriado* = *I **have** a cold*. Entonces: *I am cold* (temperatura) pero *I have a cold* (enfermedad). Y un falso amigo: *constipated* **no** significa *constipado* (resfriado). ¡Significa *estreñido*! Para *estoy constipado/a*, di *I have a cold*.

> Last Monday, Mateo woke up with a fever. His head hurt, he had a sore throat, and he couldn't eat his breakfast. Lucía called Ms. Rivera at the Lakeview Hotel. "I'm sorry. I can't come to work today. My son is sick." "Don't worry," Ms. Rivera said. "You should stay with him. Get well soon, Mateo!"
>

He must drink a lot of water, and he shouldn't go to school this week. He can read or watch TV in bed." Then she wrote a prescription. "He has to take this medicine three times a day. >
> "Can I play soccer on Saturday?" Mateo asked. Dr. Patel smiled. "No, you can't. Not this week!"
>

>
> **Lakeview Hotel · Front Desk Rules**
>
> 1. You must wear your uniform and your name tag.
> 2. You have to arrive ten minutes before your shift.
> 3. You mustn't use your phone at the desk.
> 4. You should smile and say hello to every guest.
